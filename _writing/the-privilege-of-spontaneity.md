Spontaneity looks effortless from the outside. Someone says *let's go*, and ten minutes later they're out of the door, laughing, with no plan at all. It reads like a personality trait.

But the longer I watch it, the more it looks like infrastructure.[^1] To say yes on a whim you need a few quiet things to be true already: a safe way home, money that won't be missed, a family that won't worry, and a schedule with some slack in it.

> Being spontaneous is a bit like being generous. It's a lovely thing to be, and it's much easier when someone else has already paid for the groundwork.
{: .pullquote}

## What has to be true first

Here's the rough checklist I keep in my head. None of these are *moods*; they're conditions.

| Condition | What it buys you | Who usually pays for it |
|---|---|---|
| A safe way home | You can stay out late | Often someone else |
| Spare money | You can say yes to the extra thing | A budget, quietly |
| Free time | You can drop the plan | A job that allows it |
| No one waiting | You can change your mind | Someone's patience |

I'm not arguing that spontaneity is bad. I'm arguing that it is <mark>a lovely outcome of stability, not a substitute for it</mark>.

## What I'm trying instead

I'm not giving up on spontaneity. I'm trying to *build* the groundwork so it's available more often, for me and for the people around me. Sometimes that means lending a ride home. Sometimes it means saying *yes, and let me sort the logistics*.
